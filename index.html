120 V/10 A DC-DC Supercapacitor Charger

Citrus Racing EV Team · In progress

A synchronous half-bridge DC-DC converter rated for 120 VDC at 10 A continuous. It steps a 72 V battery array up to charge a 97 V nominal supercapacitor bank for Citrus Racing's first electric powertrain.

The supercapacitor bank is there to absorb acceleration inrush current past the roughly 10 A ceiling of the batteries' internal BMS units. A blocking diode keeps regenerative braking energy from back-feeding into the array.

IRIS: Integrated Readout for Incoming Storms

Personal PCB Project · Board V1.0 designed and reviewed

IRIS answers one question: what is the weather about to do to me, right now, where I am standing. It is not a navigation device, so GNSS sits in the design as a supporting sensor rather than the headline.

Four constraints drove every decision. Multi-day winter alpine use makes battery life the governing spec. Glove operation rules out menus and button combinations. Wet, cold, exposed terrain calls for a sealed enclosure and an external probe. And building it alone on a finite timeline pushed me toward off-the-shelf breakouts on a custom carrier board instead of fully integrated silicon.

Sensing Suite

The AHT20 and SHT20 are not redundant. The AHT20 watches the sealed electronics bay for condensation and dew point, while the SHT20 out on the external stub reads outside ambient.

GNSS Selection

I evaluated six candidates. The MAX-M10S was my first pick but went backordered, so the board uses a Quectel LC76G: about $18, antenna included, five constellations, native I²C, on-chip low-power mode.

Then I found the problem. The LC76G has no ephemeris backup battery, so losing power forces a full 26-second cold start every time. That inverts the power strategy I had planned.

Strategy Start type Cost per fix Average draw
Hard power gating (original plan) 26 s cold ~936 mAs ~1.6 mA
Software standby, VCC maintained ~1 s hot n/a <200 µA

Leaving the module powered costs roughly eight times less energy than switching it off, because it holds ephemeris in RAM and hot-starts in about a second. GPS VCC ties straight to 3V3 on the board as designed, so getting standby working in firmware is a prerequisite for usable battery life rather than a refinement.

Power Architecture

State / item Draw Note
nRF52840 deep sleep ~2 µA Picked over ESP32-S3 (~10 µA) on this figure alone
AS3935 listening ~600 µA Always on, since a missed strike is a missed alert
BMP280 / AHT20 / SI1145 idle <5 µA Sleep between forced reads

Baseline without the ring and GPS is about 630 µA. As designed with both running, the board draws about 47 mA, which gives 4.3 days on the 5000 mAh cell, or 2.6 days derated for cold. Multi-week runtime is the target once GPS standby and ring gating are in firmware. That work is not done, so 4.3 days is the number I would quote today.

EMI Strategy

The AS3935 is the most sensitive part on the board by a wide margin, resolving 100 µV signals at 500 kHz. Most of the layout follows from protecting it.

  • Full-board ground pour on both layers with 90 stitching vias.
  • An antenna keepout zone under the lightning sensor's magnetic loop antenna, since copper underneath induces eddy currents and detunes it.
  • About 40 mm of physical separation between the motor driver transistor and the lightning sensor.
  • Routing kept almost entirely on one layer, with only two short back-layer traces on the whole board.
  • A 100 nF snubber specified at the motor terminals rather than on the PCB. Brushed DC motors emit broadband impulsive RF noise, which is exactly what the AS3935 is built to read as a lightning strike, so killing it at the source is the only placement that works.

The governing rule: never split the plane under a signal. Return current takes the lowest-inductance path directly beneath its trace, and a gap forces a detour that turns into an antenna.

Pre-Fabrication Design Review

I ran a structured review with severity triage before ordering anything. Three blockers turned up.

  • The LED ring and haptic motor were wired to VBUS instead of VBAT, so both would have been dead on battery power. Moving them to 3V3 does not fix it either: WS2812 dies have different forward voltages (red around 2.0 V, green and blue around 3.0 to 3.2 V) and a logic-high threshold of 0.7×VDD, which at 5 V works out to 3.5 V and sits out of reach for 3.3 V logic. VBAT works across the whole discharge curve and moves the 720 mA full-white transient off the sensor rail.
  • The power switch ran through a GND pad, which routed the entire board's ground return through the module's internal copper: thin traces never meant to carry motor and ring current, sitting directly under the radio. I corrected it to high-side switching. The dangerous part is that it would have worked, and no ERC or DRC rule flags it.
  • The original board had no decoupling capacitors at all. Partly resolved now with a 330 µF aluminum polymer cap at the ring connector. Polymer rather than electrolytic, because electrolytics lose 40 to 60% of their capacitance and pick up roughly 10× the ESR at −20 °C, which is the condition this device exists for.

The review also turned up a floating GPS reset pin, the same class of defect as the floating !RST that made it into fabricated hardware on Apogee. This time it was caught before the board was ordered.

Enclosure

Two-part PETG shell, picked over PLA for cold toughness and layer adhesion, with a gasketed lip-and-channel seam, a polycarbonate display window (acrylic embrittles below freezing), and heat-set inserts.

The requirement I did not see coming: a fully sealed enclosure makes the barometer measure trapped air. Cooling from 20 °C to −20 °C moves internal pressure from about 1013 hPa to 875 hPa with no weather change at all. Firmware would read that 138 hPa swing as roughly 1200 m of altitude gain and an incoming storm, every time the device went outside. A Gore-type ePTFE vent patch fixes it, designed in now rather than cut into a finished print later.

Deferred: Wind Speed

Six approaches evaluated: cup and propeller (bulky, moving parts freeze), hot-wire CTA (fragile 5 µm wire), ultrasonic time-of-flight (best on the merits, too bulky for a slim stub), calorimetric thermopile (FS3000, sealed, about $15, capped at 15 m/s), pitot (not sensitive enough at hiking speeds), and micro impeller (about $95, uncommon).

The FS3000's 15 m/s ceiling clips conditions I would actually be out in, since Adirondack summit gusts run 12 to 25 m/s. Rather than stall the build, the external stub uses a keyed multi-pin connector and the firmware data field is already reserved, so an FS3000 in a printed duct drops in later.

Open Items

  • Widen the VBAT trace from 0.3 mm to 0.8–1.0 mm now that it carries ring current, and the 3V3 trace from 0.2 mm to 0.4–0.5 mm over its 125 mm run.
  • Measure actual I²C bus pull-up resistance with all modules attached before deciding whether to populate R2/R3. Six Gravity breakouts each carry their own ~10 kΩ pull-up, which calculates to about 1.7 kΩ in parallel.
  • Find the LC76G standby command in the full Quectel protocol spec, since it decides whether battery life is days or weeks.
  • Grow the enclosure. The board is 102 mm against a 95 mm enclosure design.

Apogee: Fully Custom Mechanical Keyboard PCB

Latency Optimization

The first firmware worked but dropped key presses above about 5 clicks per second. I built the latency budget up from I²C bit timing. One write is 29 bits minimum (start, address byte, ACK, register pointer, ACK, data byte, ACK, stop) and a full scan is 16 columns at 2 writes each.

Three changes followed: I²C bus from 100 kHz to 400 kHz, debounce window from 5 ms to 2 ms, BLE poll interval from 15 ms to 7.5 ms.

Unoptimized Optimized
Per I²C write 0.29 ms 0.073 ms
Full matrix scan 9.28 ms 2.34 ms
USB end-to-end ≈9.8 ms ≈2.8 ms
BLE end-to-end ≈16.8 ms ≈6.05 ms

To check the math I built an Arduino dual-probe rig, one probe on a switch terminal and one on a spare Nice!Nano pin, timing switch closure to GPIO toggle across 100 presses. Minimum 0.72 ms, maximum 7.82 ms, mean 2.71 ms, median 2.51 ms. Close to the calculation, though not exact.

One tradeoff shaped the whole design. Two MCU pins bought 16 I/O, the display, and the expansion port through the I²C expander, and that same I²C path then became the dominant term in the latency budget.

Battery Life

Modeled per component per state. Every 1 mA drawn at the 5.2 V rail costs about 1.6 mA from the 3.7 V battery.

Measured in normal daily use: about 6 hours, against a 4 hour requirement.

Testing & Verification

  • Keystroke error rate: 5,000 characters of known text typed, with no incorrect or dropped characters.
  • Fast typing: no key drops at a measured 10 clicks per second, up from the 5 per second failure point before optimization.
  • End-to-end latency: the 100-trial dual-probe measurement above.
Floating reset pin

The MCP23017's !RST pin was left floating, and DRC never caught it, because the connection was missing from the schematic entirely rather than miswired. I found it during bring-up and fixed it with a jumper wire that is still on the board.

The root cause was a process gap rather than carelessness. DRC validates consistency, not intent, which is why design review has to be a separate step.

Other Found Issues

  • The drill file never made it to the fab. Boards came back with no drilled holes, which forced improvised fixes for the spacebar stabilizer and cost weeks of testing options.
  • No dedicated I²C pull-ups. The bus works only because the specific OLED module happens to include its own, so a different module would break the board.
  • The TRRS expansion port has no hot-plug protection. Contacts momentarily short 3.3 V, GND, SDA, and SCL during insertion.
  • No accessible test points, which made bring-up slower because probe leads had to be held against IC pins directly.

Routing Retrospective

  • Scan axis and diode orientation is the largest improvement available. Flipping diode direction would let me scan 5 rows instead of 16 columns, which is 10 writes per scan instead of 32, with row reads never traversing I²C.
  • I chose 2 layers over 4 mostly arbitrarily, which left no ground plane. It caused no problem at 400 kHz, but by my own written assessment it worked via luck, not design margin.
  • BLE antenna keep-out never entered the layout. Nothing failed because of it, but it is basic practice I skipped.

Lessons & V2 Plan

The big one: calculations should drive part selection, and I did it backwards, picking parts first and working out their design impact afterward. For V2 I want to calculate before buying, run more than one design review, test modules individually before integration, and put IC sockets and test points everywhere from the start.

HockeyBot: Autonomous Air Hockey Robot

Undergraduate Capstone · 4-person team · ECS Open House 2026

A robotic 1v1 opponent on a 20" × 40" air hockey table. It intercepts pucks hit at gesture speed, localizes a 2.5" puck to within 1.25", and plays offensive and defensive moves that get harder as the human scores. Total new parts cost was $294 against a $300 cap.

Full-assembly CAD in Fusion 360, across roughly three complete system revisions plus many more on individual sub-assemblies.

System Architecture

Test Results

Test 1 set a bar of 70% block accuracy on shots under 1.2 m/s at max difficulty. Three players took 20 shots each, played like a real game with no distinction between good and bad trajectories.

Tester Saves Goals Rate
Person 1 18 2 90%
Person 2 17 3 85%
Person 3 19 1 95%

All three cleared the bar individually, 54 of 60 saves overall. Tests 2 and 3 covered strategic offensive and defensive modes and difficulty scaling. Test 3 was logged as a 2-of-3 pass: the third planned difficulty lever, deliberately selecting an incorrect targeting line at low difficulty, got dropped because the robot's natural mechanical imprecision already made easy modes easy enough.

Hardware Design

I owned the mechanical structure, motors, and power systems. Full assembly CAD in Fusion 360, then machined and assembled by hand.

  • Frame architecture built around a dedicated 1.5" aluminum extrusion mounting rail bolted to each side of the table, backed with plates acting as oversized washers so the bolts could be torqued hard without pulling through the table's weak MDF. A dedicated frame, rather than bolting components straight to the table, keeps every mounting point square and parallel to the others and stops alignment drift.
  • CoreXY belt routing has to stay as parallel to the guide rails as possible, since any deviation introduces error against the underlying equations of motion. Two of the four runs achieved near-perfect parallelism. The other two were harder and remain an improvement area rather than something I would call solved.
  • The paddle head both strikes the puck and terminates all four belts: two bearings bolted together at the middle, with belts wrapping the circular midsection and exiting through a side slot so the teeth overlap for mechanical strength.
Failure loop, motor thermals

The original 3D-printed PLA motor mounts melted under motor heat. I reprinted them in PETG as a backup, then machined replacements from 1/8" aluminum sheet, cut slightly larger than the motor, with a center hole for a 1/4-20 bolt to the mounting rail and corner holes into the motor's own mounting points.

That stopped the melting, and lifting the motor off a solid molded body onto an open bolted plate also improved airflow, so the mount doubled as a heatsink. The motors were still too hot to touch, so I added forced air, tested several fan types against each other, and settled on a 12 VDC squirrel-cage blower per motor.

A borrowed best practice that did not transfer

My early belt-tension references came from 3D-printer tuning communities, where the convention is to tension belts guitar-string tight. Here that convention actively hurts, for two reasons I worked out. 3D printers follow pre-planned G-code that minimizes sudden direction changes, while this robot reacts live to an unpredictable puck with frequent hard reversals at speed, which is far more instantaneous load. And the idler-wheel mounts had a design flaw: the bearing shoulder at 4 mm was narrower than the washers bearing against it, so they lacked compression isolation, a way to bolt something down tight without binding the bearing's rotation.

The tradeoff nets out like this. More tension means less belt-tooth slip but more idler resistance and motor stalling from insufficient torque margin. Less tension gives the reverse. I built and tested several tensioning mechanisms chasing the balance point.

Motor Selection

I started by testing the lab's existing Pololu steppers against the requirement, 4 in-oz at 900 RPM or better with 2× margin, to avoid spending money I did not need to. They topped out at 500 to 700 RPM from a standing start and only reached about 1000 RPM with a 10-second acceleration ramp, nowhere near adequate for reactive play. That justified sourcing 8-wire Anaheim 23Y motors that could be wired series for torque or parallel for speed.

  • Measured actual RMS against peak coil current with a Hantek CC-65 current probe, clamped around two coil leads together. Clamping a single wire returns a falsely halved reading, a measurement error I caught and corrected.
  • Swept driver current limits from 2 A to 9 A.
  • Tested series and parallel wiring configurations.
  • Swept pulse widths from 660 µs down to 450 µs.
  • Swept supply voltage from 24 V to 30 V.
  • Eliminated every combination that stalled, missed steps, or exceeded the motor's rated RMS current.

The surviving configuration was parallel wiring, an 8 A driver limit, and a fast ramp from 700 µs to 200 µs in well under 0.5 s. The 30 V sweet spot came out of the same process: I swept voltage upward only until the system had enough headroom to move fast without binding, rather than defaulting to a supply's rated maximum.

I also validated protection by inducing the fault on purpose. Hand-stalling a spinning motor confirmed the driver's overcurrent shutdown triggered after about 4 seconds and recovered cleanly after a power cycle.

Vibration Analysis

The overhead camera mount is effectively an inverted pendulum over the table, which raised the question of whether the table legs could feed lateral vibration up the arm and amplify it. Rather than guess, I modeled it with the wobble and resonance equations. The system's resonant frequency damps the vibrations present in this application instead of amplifying them. Combined with mounting to both sides of the table, that ruled wobble out, so it became a fix I avoided building.

Takeaway

What limited continuous play was stepper motor binding after roughly 1 to 2 minutes at high speed, a motor-selection constraint that software could not overcome. The mechanical system itself ran several hours without issue in its final revision. The report's primary conclusion: actuator performance sets a hard ceiling on what the surrounding software can achieve, and that ceiling should be identified early.

Alfred: Search & Rescue Robot

CSE 400 Robotics Programming Lab · Team project · ~2 months

The assignment: navigate both rooms of an unmapped lab through standard single doors, search for a box of random height at a random location, retrieve it, and return it home. Fully autonomous, with no manual control at any stage.

Hardware

Drive
Two front drive wheels, each with an encoder, plus a free-spinning rear caster
Manipulator
2-joint arm with a simple hook end effector
Sensing
One standard USB webcam, with no LIDAR and no depth camera
Compute
RockPi
Stack
ROS 2 Iron with Nav2, live SLAM

All navigation ran on wheel encoders plus that single camera and AprilTags. The room was not pre-mapped, so the robot had to SLAM live.

AprilTags, Two Purposes

  • Localization: about 15 to 20 tags at known fixed positions across two rooms, fed to the robot as ground truth. Encoder drift, wheel slip, and IMU inaccuracy degraded localization quickly, so the tags provided constant re-confirmation.
  • Target identification: one tag on the box itself. Unlike the fixed tags this one moved, and its ID encoded which of three box heights it was, selecting the pickup routine.

We modeled the room in Blender to derive accurate XY reference coordinates for every tag placement, then used that same model to generate the map fed to the robot. CAD as a measurement tool rather than a design tool.

The generated navigation map, derived from a Blender model of the lab.

Pickup Sequence

A fixed search path covered both rooms and interrupted the moment the box was detected. The box's tag ID set arm height and approach positioning. Once vision-aligned, the robot drove forward a fixed distance to get the hook under a loop on the box top, raised the arm, and drove off.

The dominant problem: keeping localization

The onboard sensors were cheap, so localization degraded quickly whenever the robot was not actively looking at an AprilTag. The camera made it worse, since an inexpensive USB webcam could not reliably detect tags across a room.

The fix was distance-gated tag rejection: discard tag detections beyond a threshold distance rather than trusting readings the camera physically could not resolve. Both fixes came from hardware limitations rather than software bugs.

Result

Alfred was the only robot in the class to achieve a fully autonomous search-and-retrieve run. The next-best team had to manually drive their robot to the box and back, despite autonomy being a stated requirement.

Ctrl C + Ctrl Pi: Smart Copy Machine

Junior Year Design Project · Complete

A machine that draws pictures, replicates hand-drawn images, and colors in objects. Computer vision plus stepper motors move a pen over a whiteboard, with additional steppers controlling the whiteboard's height.

Hardware

X and Y for pen motion on a belt-driven carriage, one stepper each; Z for whiteboard height, 2 steppers on a screw mechanism
Motors
4 steppers, 3 × MP6500 driver chips
Vision
USB 3.0 webcam
Compute
Raspberry Pi 4 Model B
Structure
8 linear ball bearings, 13 hardened guide rods, 8 CNC-machined aluminum corners, GT2 belts and pulleys, custom 3D-printed mounts

Software Pipeline

When the user finishes drawing, the camera captures a frame that triggers the image-processing script. The image gets processed and skeletonized, leaving contours that convert into XY coordinates. Those go into an ordered list forming the pen path, including breakpoints marking where the pen has to lift for disconnected lines.

Coordinates become motor movement by pulsing GPIO pins, sending step counts and direction data to the MP6500 drivers, with Bresenham's algorithm handling smooth diagonal movement. Stack: Python with PIL, OpenCV, scikit-image, NumPy, Matplotlib, and RPi.GPIO.

ApertureAnalysis

Computational Physics Independent Study · Fall 2024 · MIT license

A semester-long independent study under computational physics professor Walter Freeman, which I took on specifically to learn the mathematics and programming of the FFT algorithm from scratch. His role was explaining math and concepts; he did not contribute to or review the code.

The Physics

A telescope's diffraction pattern, the star-shaped glare around bright stars that shows up most recognizably in JWST images, is mathematically the 2D Fourier transform of its aperture: the mirror shape, including the struts holding any secondary mirror. Sharp edges and straight struts produce straight spikes, each at an angle corresponding to an edge or strut orientation.

This program runs that relationship backward. Take a photo of a bright star, 2D FFT it, find the angles of the strongest spikes, and reconstruct the polygon aperture that produced them. JWST's hexagonal segments yield 6 principal spike directions. A camera lens yields a polygon matching its aperture blade count.

Pipeline

File Language Role
readImage.py Python Loads the JPG, crops to power-of-2 dimensions, dumps raw RGB pixel data as text
2dFFT.c++ C++ Reads that text, runs the custom 2D FFT, finds spike angles, prints them
reconstructAperture.py Python Reads spike angles and intensities, draws the reconstructed polygonal aperture
createImg.py Python Utility that turns pixel-data-as-text back into a viewable image, with optional log/HDR scaling

The two sides communicate over plain text through stdin and stdout pipes, with no shared library or binary format.

The C++ Core

  • Reads pixel text into three width × height complex arrays for R, G, and B.
  • Runs a custom recursive radix-2 Cooley–Tukey FFT on every row, transposes, runs it again on the now-transposed rows, and transposes back. The standard separable-FFT approach for 2D, with no FFTW and no numpy.fft.
  • Calls fftShift() so the zero-frequency term sits at image center rather than split across the four corners.
  • Computes each pixel's angle from image center and sums FFT magnitude into 360 one-degree bins.
  • Thresholds those bins, defaulting to above 56% of the peak bin, to separate spikes from background, then prints angle and intensity pairs to stdout with debug output routed to stderr.

A full IFFT is implemented as well, and logarithmic high-dynamic-range scaling makes the magnitude spectra render meaningfully instead of as a single saturated point.

Validation

Tested on real images throughout: actual JWST photographs, and photos from my own camera and lens setups. The second set matters because I could physically inspect my own equipment's aperture blade count and check the reconstruction against it, rather than relying only on published mirror geometry. The program produced correct results on every case I tested.

Documented limitations

The radix-2 FFT only accepts power-of-2 dimensions, so the auto-crop can silently discard image content. Spike detection is angle-only and radius-agnostic. Only the red channel is analyzed by default. The 0.56 peak-detection threshold is a hand-tuned constant calibrated on specific test images and will not generalize, which the README says outright along with where to change it. Polygon inference is heuristic rather than geometric modeling: it assumes a regular polygon from the most common angular gap, which works for symmetric hex and octagonal apertures but would be fooled by an irregular one.

Writing the FFT by hand was purely a learning goal. I wanted to understand why the algorithm works, and to experiment with which implementation changes produced the most improvement against which operations dominated runtime.
