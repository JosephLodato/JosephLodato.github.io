A wearable weather-alert system that answers exactly one question: what is the weather about to do to me, right now, where I am standing. Deliberately not a navigation device or a fitness tracker — GNSS is a supporting sensor, not the headline feature.

GNSS Selection — The Non-Obvious Result

Six candidates were evaluated. The MAX-M10S was the first choice but went backordered; the selected Quectel LC76G costs ~$18, includes an antenna, supports 5 constellations natively over I²C, and has an on-chip low-power mode.

The critical finding came afterward: unlike the MAX-M10S, the LC76G has no ephemeris backup battery, so any power loss forces a full 26-second cold start. That inverts the intended power strategy.

Strategy Start type Cost per fix Average draw
Hard power gating (original plan) 26 s cold ~936 mAs ~1.6 mA
Software standby, VCC maintained ~1 s hot — <200 µA

Leaving the module powered uses roughly 8× less energy than switching it off, because the module holds ephemeris in RAM. The board is designed with GPS VCC tied directly to 3V3, so getting standby working in firmware is a prerequisite for acceptable battery life, not an optimization.

Baseline without ring and GPS is ~630 µA. As designed with both always on: ~47 mA → ~4.3 days on the 5000 mAh cell, or ~2.6 days cold-derated. With GPS standby and ring gating implemented in firmware, multi-week is the target — but that firmware work hasn't happened yet, so 4.3 days is the honest current number.

EMI Strategy

The AS3935 is by far the most sensitive part on the board, detecting ~100 µV signals at 500 kHz. Everything about the layout follows from protecting it:

  • Full-board ground pour on both layers with 90 stitching vias.
  • An antenna keepout zone under the lightning sensor's magnetic loop antenna — copper underneath induces eddy currents and detunes it.
  • ~40 mm of physical separation between the motor driver transistor and the lightning sensor.
  • Routing kept almost entirely on one layer — only two short back-layer traces on the whole board.
  • A 100 nF snubber specified at the motor terminals, not on the PCB. Brushed DC motors emit broadband impulsive RF noise, which is precisely what the AS3935 is built to interpret as a lightning strike; killing it at the source is the only placement that works.

The governing rule, stated explicitly in the design notes: never split the plane under a signal. Return current wants the lowest-inductance path directly beneath its trace; a gap forces a detour, and that detour becomes an antenna.

Pre-Fabrication Design Review

I ran a structured review with severity triage before ordering any board. Three blockers were caught:

  • LED ring and haptic motor wired to VBUS instead of VBAT — both would have been dead on battery power. The fix isn't simply "use 3V3": WS2812 dies have different forward voltages (red ~2.0 V vs. green/blue ~3.0–3.2 V) and a logic-high threshold of 0.7×VDD, which at 5 V is 3.5 V — unreachable from 3.3 V logic. VBAT works across the entire discharge curve and moves the 720 mA full-white transient off the sensor rail.
  • Power switch routed through a GND pad, which put the entire board's ground return through the module's internal copper — thin traces never intended to carry motor and ring current, running directly beneath the radio. Corrected to high-side switching. The dangerous part is that it would have worked; no ERC or DRC rule flags it.
  • Zero decoupling capacitors on the original board. Partially resolved — a 330 µF aluminum polymer cap at the ring connector, specifically not electrolytic, because electrolytics lose 40–60% capacitance and gain ~10× ESR at −20 °C, exactly the conditions this device exists for.
Why this matters

The review also found a floating GPS reset pin — the same class of defect as the floating !RST pin that reached fabricated hardware on Apogee, caught this time before the board was ordered instead of during bring-up. That contrast is the entire argument for design review as a step separate from automated checking.

Enclosure — A Physics Requirement, Not a Nice-to-Have

Two-part PETG shell chosen over PLA for cold toughness and layer adhesion, with a gasketed lip-and-channel seam, a polycarbonate display window (not acrylic — acrylic embrittles below freezing), and heat-set inserts.

The requirement I didn't expect: a fully sealed enclosure makes the barometer measure trapped air. Cooling from 20 °C to −20 °C alone shifts internal pressure from ~1013 hPa to ~875 hPa with zero actual weather change — a 138 hPa swing the firmware would read as ~1200 m of phantom altitude gain and an incoming storm, every single time the device goes outside. The fix is a Gore-type ePTFE vent patch, designed in now rather than cut into a finished print later.

Deferred: Wind Speed

Six approaches were evaluated — cup/propeller (bulky, freezes), hot-wire CTA (fragile 5 µm wire), ultrasonic time-of-flight (technically best, too bulky for a slim stub), calorimetric thermopile (FS3000, sealed, ~$15, but hard-capped at 15 m/s), pitot (insufficient sensitivity at hiking speeds), and micro impeller (~$95, uncommon).

The FS3000's 15 m/s ceiling clips real conditions — Adirondack summit gusts run 12–25 m/s. Rather than stall the build, the external stub uses a keyed multi-pin connector and the firmware data field is already reserved, so an FS3000-in-a-printed-duct module drops straight in later. The general principle: make the interface modular, defer the hard problem, don't let it stall the build.

Open Items

  • Widen the VBAT trace (0.3 mm → 0.8–1.0 mm, now carrying ring current) and the 3V3 trace (0.2 mm → 0.4–0.5 mm over a 125 mm run).
  • Measure actual I²C bus pull-up resistance with all modules attached before deciding whether to populate R2/R3 — six Gravity breakouts each carry their own ~10 kΩ pull-up, calculated to ~1.7 kΩ in parallel.
  • Find the LC76G standby command in the full Quectel protocol spec — it determines whether battery life is days or weeks.
  • Grow the enclosure: the board is 102 mm against a 95 mm enclosure design.

Latency Optimization

The initial firmware worked but dropped key presses above ~5 clicks per second. I derived the latency budget from I²C bit timing upward: one write is 29 bits minimum — start, address byte, ACK, register pointer, ACK, data byte, ACK, stop — and a full scan is 16 columns × 2 writes.

Three changes: I²C bus 100 kHz → 400 kHz, debounce window 5 ms → 2 ms, BLE poll interval 15 ms → 7.5 ms.

Unoptimized Optimized
Per I²C write 0.29 ms 0.073 ms
Full matrix scan 9.28 ms 2.34 ms
USB end-to-end ≈9.8 ms ≈2.8 ms
BLE end-to-end ≈16.8 ms ≈6.05 ms

Measured validation: an Arduino dual-probe rig — one probe on a switch terminal, one on a spare Nice!Nano pin — timed switch closure to GPIO toggle across 100 key presses. Minimum 0.72 ms, maximum 7.82 ms, mean 2.71 ms, median 2.51 ms. The calculation was close, but not perfect, and that's what the data says.

Worth naming the tradeoff explicitly: two MCU pins bought 16 I/O, the display, and the expansion port via the I²C expander — and that same I²C path became the dominant term in the latency budget.

Testing & Verification

  • Keystroke error rate: 5,000 characters of known text typed — zero incorrect or dropped characters.
  • Fast typing: zero key drops at a measured 10 clicks/second, up from the ~5/sec failure point before optimization.
  • End-to-end latency: the 100-trial dual-probe measurement above.
The failure worth telling

The MCP23017's !RST pin was left floating — and DRC never caught it, because the connection was missing from the schematic entirely rather than miswired. I diagnosed it during bring-up and fixed it with a jumper wire that's still visible on the board.

The root cause is a process gap, not carelessness, and it's exactly why design review has to exist as a step separate from automated checking: DRC validates consistency, not intent.

Other Found Issues

  • Drill file not exported to fab. The boards came back with no drilled holes, forcing improvised fixes for the spacebar stabilizer and costing weeks of testing options.
  • No dedicated I²C pull-ups. The bus works only because the specific OLED module happens to include its own — a real design dependency that a different module would break.
  • Unprotected TRRS expansion port. No hot-plug protection; contacts momentarily short 3.3 V, GND, SDA, and SCL during insertion.
  • No accessible test points. Bring-up took longer because probe leads had to be held against IC pins directly.

Routing Retrospective

  • Scan axis / diode orientation is the single largest available improvement. Flipping diode direction would allow scanning 5 rows instead of 16 columns — 10 writes per scan instead of 32, with row reads never traversing I²C at all.
  • 2-layer vs. 4-layer was chosen mostly arbitrarily, leaving no ground plane. It caused no problem at 400 kHz, but by my own written assessment it "worked via luck," not design margin.
  • BLE antenna keep-out wasn't considered during layout. No functional issues recorded, but it's basic practice I skipped.

Lessons & V2 Plan

The biggest one: calculations drive part selection, not the inverse. I selected parts first and calculated their design-spec impact afterward, which is backwards. V2: calculate before buying, run multiple design reviews, test individual modules before integration, and plan for problems up front with IC sockets and test points everywhere.

My Contribution — Hardware Design

Full assembly CAD in Fusion 360, then machined and assembled by hand across roughly three complete system revisions.

  • Frame architecture. Built around a dedicated 1.5" aluminum extrusion mounting rail bolted to each side of the table, backed with plates acting as oversized washers so bolts could be torqued hard without pulling through the table's weak MDF. A dedicated frame — rather than bolting components straight to the table — keeps every mounting point square and parallel to every other one, preventing alignment drift.
  • CoreXY belt-routing precision. Belt segments must stay as parallel to the guide rails as possible; any deviation introduces error against the underlying equations of motion. Two of four runs achieved near-perfect parallelism. The other two were meaningfully harder and remain an improvement area rather than something I'd claim as solved.
  • Paddle head. The central carriage both strikes the puck and terminates all four belts — two bearings bolted together at the middle, belts wrapping the circular midsection and exiting through a side slot so the teeth overlap for mechanical strength.
Failure loop — motor thermals

The original 3D-printed PLA motor mounts melted under motor heat. I reprinted them in PETG as a backup, then machined replacements from 1/8" aluminum sheet — cut slightly larger than the motor, with a center hole for a 1/4-20 bolt to the mounting rail and corner holes into the motor's own mounting points.

That stopped the melting and, by lifting the motor off a solid molded body onto an open bolted plate, measurably improved airflow — the mount doubling as a heatsink. Still too hot to touch, so I added forced air, testing multiple fan types comparatively and settling on a 12 VDC squirrel-cage blower per motor.

A borrowed best practice that didn't transfer

My early belt-tension references came from 3D-printer tuning communities, where the convention is to tension belts guitar-string tight. Here that convention actively hurts, for two reasons I identified: 3D printers follow pre-planned G-code that minimizes sudden direction changes, while this robot reacts live to an unpredictable puck with frequent hard reversals at speed — far more instantaneous load. And the idler-wheel mounts had a design flaw: the bearing shoulder (4 mm) was narrower than the washers bearing against it, so they lacked compression isolation — a way to bolt something down tight without binding the bearing's rotation.

The net tradeoff: more tension means less belt-tooth slip but more idler resistance and motor stalling from insufficient torque margin. Less tension, the reverse. I built and tested multiple tensioning mechanisms chasing the balance point.

Motor Selection — Systematic Parameter Sweep

I started by testing the lab's existing Pololu steppers against the requirement (4 in-oz at ≥900 RPM, targeting 2× margin) to avoid unnecessary spend. They topped out at 500–700 RPM from a standing start and only reached ~1000 RPM with a 10-second acceleration ramp — nowhere near adequate for reactive play. That justified sourcing 8-wire Anaheim 23Y motors that could be wired series (higher torque) or parallel (higher speed).

  • Measured actual RMS vs. peak coil current with a Hantek CC-65 current probe, clamped around two coil leads together — clamping a single wire returns a falsely halved reading, a measurement error I caught and corrected.
  • Swept driver current limits from 2 A to 9 A.
  • Tested both series and parallel wiring configurations.
  • Swept pulse widths from 660 µs down to 450 µs.
  • Swept supply voltage from 24 V to 30 V.
  • Eliminated every combination that stalled, missed steps, or exceeded the motor's rated RMS current.

The surviving configuration: parallel wiring, 8 A driver limit, and a fast ramp (700 µs → 200 µs in well under 0.5 s). The 30 V "sweet spot" came from the same process — voltage swept upward only until the system had enough headroom to move fast without binding, rather than defaulting to a supply's rated maximum.

I also validated protection by intentionally inducing the fault: hand-stalling a spinning motor confirmed the driver's overcurrent shutdown triggered after ~4 seconds and recovered cleanly after a power cycle.

Vibration Analysis — Math Before Fabrication

The overhead camera mount is effectively an inverted pendulum over the table, raising the concern that table legs could feed lateral vibration up the arm and amplify it. Rather than guessing, I modeled it with the actual wobble/resonance equations, which showed the system's resonant frequency damps the vibrations present in this application rather than amplifying them. Combined with mounting to both sides of the table, wobble was ruled out — a fix avoided rather than built.

Takeaway

The limiter on continuous play was stepper motor binding after roughly 1–2 minutes of high-speed play — identified explicitly as a motor-selection constraint software could not overcome. The mechanical system itself ran several hours without issue in its final revision. The primary stated takeaway from the report: actuator performance sets a hard ceiling on what the surrounding software can achieve, and that ceiling should be identified early in the design process.

Alfred — Search & Rescue Robot

CSE 400 Robotics Programming Lab · Team project

StatusComplete
Duration~2 months

Navigate both rooms of an unmapped lab through standard single doors, search for a box of random height (three possibilities) at a random location, retrieve it, and return it home — fully autonomously, with no manual control at any stage.

Ownership

There was no individual role split on this one — the whole team worked cooperatively across every code section together. It's presented here as a team accomplishment.

Hardware

Drive
Two front drive wheels, each with an encoder, plus a free-spinning rear caster
Manipulator
2-joint arm with a simple hook end effector
Sensing
One standard USB webcam — no LIDAR, no depth camera
Compute
RockPi
Stack
ROS 2 Iron with Nav2, live SLAM

All navigation ran on wheel encoders plus that single camera and AprilTags. The room was not pre-mapped, so the robot had to SLAM live.

AprilTags — Two Distinct Purposes

  • Localization: ~15–20 tags at known fixed positions across two rooms, fed to the robot as ground truth. These were necessary rather than optional — encoder drift, wheel slip, and IMU inaccuracy degraded localization quickly, so the tags provided constant re-confirmation.
  • Target identification: one tag on the box itself. Unlike the fixed tags, this one moved, and its ID encoded which of three box heights it was, selecting the pickup routine.

We modeled the room in Blender to derive accurate XY reference coordinates for every tag placement, and used that same model to generate the map fed to the robot — CAD as a measurement tool rather than a design tool.

The generated navigation map, derived from a Blender model of the lab.

Pickup Sequence

A fixed search path covered both rooms and interrupted the moment the box was detected. The box's tag ID determined arm height and approach positioning; once vision-aligned, the robot drove forward a fixed distance to get the hook under a loop on the box top, raised the arm, and drove off.

The dominant problem: keeping localization

The onboard sensors were cheap, so localization degraded quickly whenever the robot wasn't actively looking at an AprilTag. The camera compounded this — an inexpensive USB webcam couldn't reliably detect tags across a room.

The fix was distance-gated tag rejection: discard tag detections beyond a threshold distance rather than trusting readings the camera physically couldn't resolve. Both fixes were driven by hardware limitations rather than software bugs — a distinction worth drawing.

Result

Alfred was the only robot in the class to achieve a fully autonomous search-and-retrieve run. The next-best team had to manually drive their robot to the box and back, despite autonomy being a stated requirement.

Software Pipeline

When the user finishes drawing, the camera captures a frame that triggers the image-processing script. The image is processed and skeletonized, leaving contours that convert into XY coordinates. ApertureAnalysis

Computational Physics Independent Study · Fall 2024 · Fully solo
OwnershipFully solo
StatusComplete
Duration~2 months
LicenseMIT

A semester-long independent study under computational physics professor Walter Freeman, undertaken specifically to learn the mathematics and programming of the FFT algorithm from scratch. His role was explaining math and concepts; he did not contribute to or review the code.

The Physics

A telescope's diffraction pattern — the star-shaped glare around bright stars, most recognizably in JWST images — is mathematically the 2D Fourier transform of its aperture: the mirror shape, including the struts holding any secondary mirror. Sharp edges and straight struts produce straight spikes, each lying at an angle corresponding to an edge or strut orientation.

This program runs that relationship backward: take a photo of a bright star → 2D FFT it → find the angles of the strongest spikes → reconstruct the polygon aperture that produced them. JWST's hexagonal segments yield 6 principal spike directions; a camera lens yields a polygon matching its aperture blade count.

Pipeline

File Language Role
readImage.py Python Loads the JPG, crops to power-of-2 dimensions, dumps raw RGB pixel data as text
2dFFT.c++ C++ Reads that text, runs the custom 2D FFT, finds spike angles, prints them
reconstructAperture.py Python Reads spike angles and intensities, draws the reconstructed polygonal aperture
createImg.py Python Utility — turns pixel-data-as-text back into a viewable image, with optional log/HDR scaling

The two sides communicate over plain text through stdin/stdout pipes — no shared library, no binary format.

The C++ Core

  • Reads pixel text into three width × height complex arrays (R, G, B).
  • Runs a custom recursive radix-2 Cooley–Tukey FFT on every row, transposes, runs it again on the now-transposed rows, and transposes back — the standard separable-FFT approach for 2D. No FFTW, no numpy.fft.
  • fftShift()s the result so the zero-frequency term sits at image center rather than split across the four corners.
  • Computes each pixel's angle from image center and sums FFT magnitude into 360 one-degree bins.
  • Thresholds those bins (default: above 56% of the peak bin) to separate real spikes from background, then prints angle/intensity pairs to stdout with debug output routed to stderr.

A full IFFT is implemented as well, and high-dynamic-range logarithmic scaling makes the magnitude spectra render meaningfully rather than as a single saturated point.

Validation

Tested on real images throughout — actual JWST photographs and photos from my own camera and lens setups. The latter matters: I could physically inspect my own equipment's aperture blade count and check the reconstruction against it, rather than relying only on published mirror geometry. The program produced correct results on all tested cases.

Documented limitations

The radix-2 FFT only accepts power-of-2 dimensions, so the auto-crop can silently discard real image content. Spike detection is angle-only and radius-agnostic. Only the red channel is analyzed by default. The 0.56 peak-detection threshold is a hand-tuned constant calibrated on specific test images and won't generalize — the README says so and documents where to change it. Polygon inference is heuristic rather than geometric modeling: it assumes a regular polygon from the most common angular gap, which works for symmetric hex/octagonal apertures but would be fooled by an irregular one.

Writing the FFT by hand was purely a learning goal — understanding why the algorithm works, and experimenting to see which implementation changes produced the most improvement versus which operations dominated runtime.
